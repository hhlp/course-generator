# ENGLISH GRAMMAR — C1 → C2

## Domain

Advanced English grammar from CEFR C1 through C2. The course is deliberately grammar-only: vocabulary, pronunciation, reading, listening, writing and speaking are not independent curriculum strands. Lexical items and contextual examples may be used only insofar as they clarify grammatical form, meaning, constraints, contrast or usage.

## Course numbering

This is an independent learning path. Block numbering starts at **Block 0** and does not inherit numbering from the general English A1→C2 curriculum.

## Scope

The curriculum develops advanced control and analysis of:

- tense and aspect;
- future reference;
- modal and semi-modal systems;
- linking verbs, predicatives and complementation;
- active/passive alternations and advanced passive constructions;
- interrogative structures;
- verb complementation and argument structure;
- direct and indirect reporting;
- nouns and complex noun phrases;
- articles, determiners and quantification;
- relative, finite and non-finite clauses;
- pronouns, substitution and ellipsis;
- adjectives, adverbs and comparison;
- prepositions and prepositional phrases;
- coordination, subordination and adverbial clauses;
- conditional, hypothetical and counterfactual structures;
- word order, fronting, inversion and emphasis;
- clefting, extraposition, existential constructions and information packaging;
- complex clause architecture, embedding and structural ambiguity;
- grammatical variation, register-sensitive grammatical choice and meaning contrasts between alternative constructions;
- negation, polarity and semantic/syntactic scope;
- definiteness, specificity, reference and quantifier scope;
- raising, control and advanced complementation diagnostics;
- constituency, attachment and competing structural analyses;
- grammaticality, acceptability and interpretation;
- descriptive vs prescriptive grammar where relevant to advanced usage.

## Level model

C1 and C2 are treated as a continuum rather than two artificially isolated grammar syllabuses. A construction may first be consolidated at C1 and subsequently revisited through finer semantic, syntactic and discourse-sensitive distinctions characteristic of C2 control.

The progression is:

1. recognise the structure;
2. understand its grammatical form;
3. understand its core meaning;
4. distinguish it from competing constructions;
5. identify syntactic constraints;
6. manipulate the construction accurately;
7. explain meaning changes produced by grammatical alternatives;
8. analyse the construction inside complex sentences;
9. diagnose grammatical errors and ambiguity;
10. exercise C1→C2 grammatical choice with precision;
11. distinguish grammaticality, acceptability, register and prescriptive preference;
12. identify scope, attachment and reference ambiguities;
13. justify competing structural analyses when more than one interpretation is possible.

## Principal reference architecture

*Advanced Grammar in Use*, 4th edition, by Martin Hewings is the principal pedagogical grammar and the main reference for sequencing the advanced grammar domain. The course does not reproduce the book verbatim. Its topics are reorganised into a progressive learning path with additional integration, grammatical analysis and mastery checkpoints.

Reference grammars are used to deepen areas where a learner needs more explicit syntactic or semantic explanation than a pedagogical grammar normally provides.

## Boundaries

### Included

Advanced grammar, grammatical terminology, sentence analysis, form–meaning contrasts, syntactic constraints, grammatical acceptability, alternative constructions, negation and polarity, scope, reference, definiteness, raising and control, constituency, ambiguity, error analysis, descriptive/prescriptive distinctions and grammar-sensitive register variation.

### Excluded as independent subjects

- vocabulary acquisition;
- pronunciation;
- phonetics and phonology;
- reading skills;
- listening skills;
- writing instruction;
- speaking instruction;
- general conversation practice;
- literary analysis;
- general composition and rhetoric.

These may appear incidentally in examples but must not displace the grammatical objective of a lesson.

## Examples and explanation

Lessons should use contrasting examples extensively. Advanced grammar is best understood by comparing forms such as:

- simple vs progressive aspect;
- present perfect vs past simple;
- modal vs semi-modal alternatives;
- active vs passive;
- -ing vs infinitival complementation;
- finite vs non-finite clauses;
- defining vs supplementary relative clauses;
- canonical vs marked word order;
- conditional vs counterfactual alternatives;
- canonical clauses vs clefts, inversion or extraposition.

Examples should demonstrate not merely whether a sentence is grammatical, but why a construction is selected and what changes when another grammatical form is chosen.

## Terminal outcome

At the end of the path, the learner should be able to analyse and manipulate advanced English grammar at C1→C2 level, explain important form–meaning relationships, distinguish closely competing constructions, recognise constraints and ambiguity, and diagnose advanced grammatical errors with appropriate terminology.

## Explicit advanced coverage and depth boundaries

The path contains 19 blocks (0–18) and 425 lessons. All original lesson
objectives are retained; revised titles distinguish overlapping objectives.

- Block 4 distinguishes raising-to-object (also analysed as exceptional case
  marking in some frameworks), reporting passives and ordinary passivisation.
  Explain the adopted analysis without treating its terminology as universal.
- Block 6 explicitly contrasts grammatical, notional and proximity agreement;
  identify restrictions and relevant British/American variation.
- Block 8 explicitly covers free/fused relatives, absolute constructions and
  verbless clauses. Contrast relatives with embedded interrogatives; distinguish
  absolute constructions with their own subject from controlled supplements.
  Explain where traditional reduction analyses and modern analyses differ.
- Block 9 distinguishes substitute one/ones from generic personal one. Its
  comparative ellipsis lesson addresses recoverability and substitution;
  Block 11 develops comparative clause structure and interpretive constraints.
- Infinitival ellipsis distinguishes an omitted complement after retained to
  from constructions allowing omission of to as well. Do not generalise such
  omission to all infinitival complements.

CEFR levels describe communicative proficiency, not a universal inventory of
syntactic terminology. Raising/control diagnostics, constituency tests and
competing parses are analytical enrichment supporting grammatical precision;
mastery of a particular theoretical framework is not a universal C2 condition.
Explain prerequisite concepts locally when later blocks develop them further.

Every lesson includes the common-errors section. Revisited topics must add new
constraints, contrasts, interpretations or integration tasks rather than repeat
an earlier lesson. The final mastery project integrates the explicit additions
as well as the original grammar domains.


## Advanced linking: explicit grammatical coverage

The expanded path preserves all 398 original lesson objectives and adds
27 lessons, retaining 19 blocks (0-18). Insertions precede each affected
checkpoint; original checkpoint numbers change, as do the final checkpoint
and project numbers. Blocks without insertions retain their numbering.

Connectors is an umbrella teaching label, not a single syntactic category.
Distinguish coordinators, subordinators, linking adjuncts, prepositional
expressions, supplements and correlative constructions. Explain differences
between reference-grammar frameworks when relevant.

- Block 10, lessons 10.21-10.24: linking adjuncts vs conjunctions; additive,
  contrastive, corrective, result and inferential meanings and restrictions.
- Block 12, lessons 12.12-12.15: concessive, causal, purposive, exceptive and
  restrictive prepositional constructions and their complement types.
- Block 13, lessons 13.19-13.28: advanced concession, unconditional clauses,
  grounds, limitation, lest, result, temporal links, correlatives, comparison,
  alternatives and reduced-clause subject constraints.
- Block 14, lessons 14.23-14.26: precaution vs condition, conditional grounds,
  counterfactual but for, contingency and factual vs hypothetical concession.
- Block 16, lessons 16.31-16.33: placement, clause boundaries, punctuation,
  attachment, reformulation, exemplification and marker-sensitive grammar.
- Block 18, lessons 18.33-18.34: cumulative analysis and error diagnosis;
  checkpoint 18.35 and final mastery project 18.36 integrate the additions.

The profile contains the explicit inventory for all 16 functional families:
addition; contrast; concession; cause and grounds; result and inference;
purpose; condition; exception and reservation; time; comparison and
alternatives; manner and appearance; exemplification; reformulation and
correction; topic and limitation; concession, balance and summary; and
conversational markers. Lesson references identify primary teaching locations;
individual forms may also need cross-references to existing lessons.

This is an open pedagogical inventory, not an exhaustive lexical dictionary
or an official CEFR allocation. Earlier-level forms remain relevant where
advanced contrasts, restrictions, scope or ambiguity supply new depth.
Formal, legal or literary forms are not inherently C2 requirements.

### Required contrasts and constraints

Compare although + finite clause with despite + noun phrase/-ing and
despite the fact that + finite clause. Contrast even though with even if,
in case with if, purpose with result, and on the contrary with on the other
hand. Distinguish causal for from prepositional for and concessive however
+ adjective/adverb from the mobile linking adjunct however.

Analyse both the semantic relation and syntactic construction. A connective
is not interchangeable with another merely because a Spanish translation
matches. In particular, the instrumental or result relations expressed by thereby
must not be reduced to a generic therefore reading; thereby normally links
the result to the preceding action or means.

Treat lest through attested finite patterns, including subjunctive and should
constructions; do not insert redundant negation mechanically. Explain that
albeit commonly introduces reduced expressions without claiming that finite
uses never occur. Concessive fronting with as/though has construction-specific
restrictions and is not ordinary subject-auxiliary inversion.

For linking adjuncts, explain sentence-initial, medial and final positions
where natural. Compare a full stop, semicolon and coordination as ways of
separating independent clauses; diagnose comma splices without banning
sentence-initial conjunctions. Teach inversion only when the actual trigger
and its scope require it: Only if you agree will we proceed, but We will
proceed only if you agree.

For correlative constructions, analyse parallelism, scope, agreement and
ellipsis. For reduced clauses, identify the understood subject and diagnose
dangling attachments. Treat lexicalised phrases, complete framing clauses
and grammatical adjuncts as different structures.

Conversational, exemplifying, topic and summary markers serve only to
illustrate grammar, attachment, scope, inference and register. They must not
become independent conversation, essay-writing or rhetoric lessons.

Use original contrast pairs, controlled transformations and error diagnosis.
The final project integrates these choices with tense, modality, negation,
reference, coordination, subordination and information structure.
